# The Solar Harvest: How Agrivoltaics and Federal Policy Are Reshaping the Future of Farming

## Key Takeaways
* The expiration or reform of the Rural Energy for America Program (REAP) in the upcoming Farm Bill vote will determine whether thousands of farmers can continue to access cost-saving solar energy, or face a return to higher operational costs.
* Agrivoltaics—the practice of co-locating solar panels with crop production or grazing—is emerging as a proven, scalable solution that can generate new revenue streams for farmers while preserving arable land for agriculture.
* Despite recent political headwinds from a new USDA administration, the economic fundamentals of on-farm solar remain overwhelmingly positive, with net-metering and falling hardware costs driving record adoption across the rural Midwest and South.

## The Rural Energy Crossroads

For nearly two decades, the Rural Energy for America Program (REAP) has been the quiet engine driving solar adoption on American farms. Established in the 2008 Farm Bill, REAP provided grants and loan guarantees to agricultural producers and rural small businesses to install renewable energy systems and make energy efficiency improvements. The program was simple, effective, and popular—so popular that the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) routinely saw applications far exceed available funding.

Then came 2025, and a seismic shift. As Canary Media reported in August 2025, newly appointed USDA Secretary Rollins moved to block taxpayer dollars specifically for solar panels installed on what the department deems "prime farmland." The rationale, according to the Secretary, was to protect the nation's most productive agricultural soils from being "permanently covered" by solar infrastructure. The directive sent shockwaves through the solar and agricultural communities, effectively halting REAP applications for projects on land classified as prime, unique, or of statewide importance.

This policy reversal, coming just as the 2024 Farm Bill expiration looms, has thrown the future of distributed rural solar into chaos. Farmers who had planned to invest savings from solar into new equipment or herd expansion now face an uncertain path forward. The stakes could not be higher: with a Farm Bill vote on the horizon, the solar industry and its rural advocates are fighting to not only restore REAP funding but to modernize the program for the realities of modern agrivoltaics.

This article provides an expert analysis of the current state of solar energy on farmland, examining the policy battles, the emerging science of agrivoltaics, and the undeniable economic logic that continues to drive farmers toward the sun.

## The Policy Battleground: REAP, the Farm Bill, and the USDA’s Land Use War

To understand the current crisis, one must first appreciate what REAP has meant for rural America. Since its inception, REAP has disbursed hundreds of millions of dollars in grants, with a significant portion directly funding solar photovoltaic installations. For a dairy farmer in Wisconsin or a row-crop farmer in Iowa, a REAP grant could cover up to 50% of the cost of a new solar array, reducing the payback period from 10 years to 5 or 6. This financial mechanism single-handedly democratized access to solar for a sector that operates on thin margins.

However, the program operates on a five-year Farm Bill cycle. The current Farm Bill—the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018—expired in 2023, and Congress has been operating on a series of short-term extensions. The next long-term Farm Bill, which will reauthorize and fund REAP, is now the primary vehicle for the program's survival or reform.

Secretary Rollins’ August 2025 directive, officially titled "Blocking Taxpayer Dollars for Solar Panels on Prime Farmland," added a new and aggressive layer to this legislative uncertainty. The USDA’s rationale is rooted in a traditionalist view of land conservation: once you put solar panels on prime soil, you remove it from agricultural production. But this argument largely ignores the rapid maturation of **agrivoltaics**—the simultaneous use of land for both solar electricity generation and agriculture.

The Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA), in its press releases, has been unequivocal in its opposition. They argue that the USDA’s policy is based on an outdated understanding of solar technology and land use. SEIA points to a growing body of research from universities like the University of Arizona and Oregon State University, showing that crops like shade-tolerant vegetables, pollinator habitats, and certain berries can thrive under elevated solar panels, all while generating electricity income for the farmer.

Make no mistake: the Farm Bill vote is a binary moment for rural solar. If the bill passes with the Rollins directive codified, it could effectively end federal support for solar on millions of acres of the nation's most productive land. If it passes with language explicitly protecting the right of farmers to install solar on non-prime lands—or even encouraging agrivoltaic research—the industry could enter a new golden age.

## The Agrivoltaic Revolution: Can We Have Our Crops and Solar, Too?

While Washington debates, a quiet revolution is taking place in fields across the country. The old narrative that solar farms and agriculture are mutually exclusive is being empirically disproven by a wave of pioneering projects.

One of the most compelling case studies comes from a Virginia nonprofit, as reported by Canary Media in October 2025. This organization is demonstrating that farmland can not only coexist with solar but can actually thrive. The project involves raising solar panels high off the ground—typically 8 to 10 feet—and spacing them in wide rows to allow for full sunlight penetration and traditional farm equipment access.

The results have been striking. On these "solar sharing" sites, researchers are documenting increased soil moisture retention in the shade of the panels (a crucial benefit during drought), reduced heat stress on livestock, and even higher yields for certain crops like lettuce and tomatoes that thrive in partial shade. The key metric is the "Land Equivalent Ratio" (LER), which measures the combined productivity of land used for both solar and agriculture. In well-designed agrivoltaic systems, the LER can exceed 1.6, meaning that 1 acre of land is producing the equivalent of 1.6 acres of useful output.

This is not a niche, academic exercise. As a May 2025 Canary Media report titled "Farmers are making bank harvesting a new crop: Solar energy" detailed, the economics are increasingly undeniable. For a farmer facing volatile commodity prices and rising input costs, a solar lease or a self-installed array represents a steady, predictable stream of income. When that array is elevated to allow for continued grazing or planting, the farmer is effectively getting paid twice: once for the electricity and once for the crop or livestock. The report highlighted a dairy farmer in Pennsylvania who, after installing agrivoltaics, saw his monthly electric bill drop from $4,000 to nearly zero, while his cows continued to graze comfortably under the panels.

From my experience covering this sector, the most significant hurdle is no longer technology or cost—it is education and permitting. Many county zoning boards still classify agrivoltaic sites as "industrial solar farms," triggering heavy-handed setback and fencing requirements that destroy the agricultural utility of the land. Reforming these local regulations, alongside bringing the USDA’s policy in line with modern science, is the single most important action needed to scale this practice.

## The Irresistible Economics: Why Farmers Are Going Solar Anyway

Even with the USDA’s newfound hostility toward prime farmland solar, the broader economic trend is unstoppable. Farmers are businesspeople first, and the numbers for on-farm solar are simply too good to ignore.

Consider the long-term trend of solar hardware costs. According to data referenced in IRENA’s annual reports and the Clean Energy is Winning charts from Canary Media, the cost of solar photovoltaic modules has fallen by over 80% in the last decade. For a farm with a large, low-slope roof on a barn or machine shed, the cost per watt of installed solar is now competitive with utility-scale projects in many regions. When combined with the 30% federal Investment Tax Credit (ITC) and net-metering policies that allow farms to sell excess power back to the grid, the financial case is compelling.

Furthermore, the clean energy transition is not a spectator sport. As the Canary Media "10 Charts" report from December 2025 illustrates, solar and wind are now beating new power demand across the U.S. grid. This is not about altruism; it is about resilience and cost control. For a farmer, being energy-independent means being insulated from volatile grid electricity prices, which are only trending upward as the grid struggles to handle increased load from data centers and electrification.

The data from SEIA’s press releases also points to a surprising political reality. While the loudest opposition to rural solar comes from certain conservative think tanks and now the USDA, the actual adoption rates tell a different story. The top states for on-farm solar installation in 2024 and 2025 are not California and New York; they are Texas, Georgia, and Indiana. Republican-leaning agricultural states are leading the charge because the economics of "keeping the lights on for less" are universal. Farmers in these states are increasingly lobbying their own representatives to protect REAP and push back against the USDA’s land-use restrictions.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What is the Rural Energy for America Program (REAP) and why does it matter for solar?
REAP is a USDA program established in the 2008 Farm Bill that provides grants (up to 50% of project cost) and loan guarantees to agricultural producers and rural small businesses for renewable energy systems (like solar) and energy efficiency improvements. It matters because it has been the primary federal mechanism making solar affordable for farmers. Its future is uncertain, pending a new Farm Bill vote and ongoing policy restrictions from the current USDA administration.

### What is agrivoltaics, and is it a real solution for farmland solar conflicts?
Yes, agrivoltaics is a proven, science-backed practice. It involves designing solar arrays specifically to allow for continued agricultural activity beneath and around them, such as grazing livestock, growing shade-tolerant crops, or establishing pollinator habitats. Research shows it can increase total land productivity (Land Equivalent Ratio >1.6) and provide new revenue streams while preserving the land for farming. The USDA’s current policy banning solar panels on "prime farmland" largely ignores the viability of well-designed agrivoltaic systems.

### Can farmers still go solar if the USDA blocks funding for prime farmland?
While the USDA’s August 2025 directive blocks REAP grants for projects on prime farmland, farmers have other options. They can still claim the 30% federal Investment Tax Credit (ITC) on their taxes. They can also install solar on non-prime land, rooftops, or marginal soils without facing the new ban. However, losing REAP grants makes projects significantly less affordable, particularly for smaller family farms with limited capital.

### How does a solar installation affect the actual value of farmland?
This is a critical question. For ground-mounted solar on non-prime land, the land is typically leased (not sold), providing a stable income stream that can actually increase the property’s asset value to an investor. For agrivoltaic installations, the value proposition is even stronger because the land remains productive for crops or livestock. However, poorly planned "solar sprawl" that damages soil or restricts future farming can depress land values. The key is proper design and a clear decommissioning plan in the lease.

### Will the next Farm Bill solve the conflict over rural solar?
It is the single most likely venue for a solution. The Farm Bill reauthorizes REAP. If Congress writes language that explicitly permits and encourages agrivoltaics, or that narrowly defines what qualifies as "prime farmland" to exclude dual-use projects, it can override the USDA’s administrative directive. However, if the Farm Bill remains stalled or passes with the current restrictions codified, the conflict will continue, likely forcing the issue to the courts.
